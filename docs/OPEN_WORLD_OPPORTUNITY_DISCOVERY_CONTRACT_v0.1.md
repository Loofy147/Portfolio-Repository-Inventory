# Open-World Opportunity Discovery — Scope and Operating Contract v0.1

**Created:** 2026-11-02  
**Status:** ACTIVE OPERATING CONTRACT  
**Owner context:** Portfolio-wide opportunity discovery; no single project is the search boundary.  
**Portfolio source of record:** `Loofy147/Portfolio-Repository-Inventory`

## 1. Scope correction

Opportunity discovery is an independent, open-world research function. It is not a Medad review, a software-product ideation session, a startup-credit directory, an Algeria-only market scan, or a portfolio-repository audit.

Its search universe is unrestricted by:
- industry or market;
- technology or implementation medium;
- geography or customer segment;
- product, service, asset, business model or transaction type;
- the user's existing repositories, capabilities or projects.

Existing projects, repositories, skills and assets are **candidate vehicles**, not search filters. A discovery must not be promoted merely because it fits an existing project. An opportunity outside the existing portfolio remains admissible.

The objective is to discover **real, capturable value**: an unmet or under-served economic need, an exploitable resource/market position, a purchasable task, a beneficial program or incentive, or another verifiable route to value. The route must be evaluated on its own economics, requirements and risks.

## 2. What counts as an opportunity

Maintain distinct opportunity classes so that unlike things are not forced into one scoring model.

### A. Demand and market opportunities

- Product opportunities: physical, digital, software, hardware, consumable, industrial, consumer, B2B, B2C, B2G or hybrid.
- Services and specialist work: operational, technical, commercial, analytical, creative, compliance-adjacent, maintenance, implementation, research, professional or managed services.
- Software, AI and automation: only where a real customer problem and payer exist; technology is an implementation option, not evidence of demand.
- Data, APIs and information products: aggregation, verification, matching, monitoring, analysis, licensing and access rights.
- Manufacturing, sourcing, import/export, distribution, resale, logistics, fulfilment and supply-chain positions.
- Procurement, tenders, subcontracting, provider-to-provider work, project capacity, and recurring or event-driven purchasing.
- Licensing, intellectual property, standards, certification-adjacent services and technology transfer, subject to the relevant legal/credential boundary.
- Asset and capacity opportunities: under-used equipment, space, inventory, infrastructure, skills, compute, distribution access or other productive assets.
- Partnerships, acquisitions, market entry, channel access and commercial alliances.
- Research commercialization, datasets, scientific/technical methods, open-source assets and sponsored research.
- Public-sector, para-public and private-sector opportunities; local and cross-border.

### B. Enablers and non-dilutive opportunities

These reduce the cost, risk or time of pursuing another opportunity. They are valuable, but are not automatically demand or a business model.

- Cloud, compute, storage, model/API, developer and research credits.
- Grants, subsidies, vouchers, tax incentives, innovation funds and public programs.
- Accelerators, incubators, competitions, prizes, bounties and challenge programs.
- Open datasets, research access, testing facilities, labs, equipment and academic/industrial partnerships.
- Partner benefits, marketplace distribution, channel access, provider programs and donated/subsidized tools.
- Public procurement access, supplier registries and programs for qualifying enterprises.
- Regulatory transition assistance and official support programs, where they create a usable benefit.

Evaluate each incentive for actual eligibility, value that can be consumed, scope, expiry, co-funding, data/IP obligations, exclusions, geographic/legal/payment constraints, application effort, integration cost and switching/lock-in cost. Advertised maximum value is not realized economic value.

### C. Capital, investment and resource-position opportunities

- Buy/sell, license, lease or acquire under-valued or under-utilized assets.
- Arbitrage in supply, distribution, pricing, capacity, timing, geography or information, where lawful and repeatable.
- Project finance, co-investment, inventory/capacity financing, revenue-sharing or asset-backed arrangements.
- Access to an existing channel, customer base, supplier relationship, production capability or right.
- Strategic opportunities that create option value, defensible access or a measurable cost reduction.

Use a separate risk and capital model. Do not treat speculative upside as established value.

## 3. Discovery anchor: economic problem/event, not an idea category

For demand-generating opportunities, start from:

**Observable economic problem/event → consequence → current handling → gap/exception → affected owner → buyer/payer → bounded intervention → acceptance/payment test.**

Useful observation classes include:
- rejection or refusal;
- delay, queue or missed deadline;
- rework, repeated handling or duplicated effort;
- mismatch, quality failure or incompatibility;
- dispute, claim, reconciliation failure or non-acceptance;
- missing, stale or conflicting evidence;
- handoff or responsibility failure;
- supplier/customer/asset incompatibility;
- decision made with poor or incomplete information;
- operational hidden loss, asset underperformance or leakage;
- expensive/irreversible error;
- unmet product/service requirement;
- changing regulation, standard, technology or market access;
- unused capability, capacity, data or distribution access.

For each, ask:
1. What actually happened?
2. How often, to whom and under what conditions?
3. What is the economic consequence if untreated?
4. Who bears the loss or benefits from fixing it?
5. Who controls the budget and can authorize purchase?
6. Who actually pays, through what mechanism, and when?
7. What is the current workaround and who already sells a solution?
8. What smallest bounded outcome could be sold and accepted?

A new technology, trend, law, product concept, patent or funding announcement can be a discovery trigger. It is not a demand verdict.

## 4. No false mandatory filters

The following can be useful signals but must never become universal requirements:

- **Sector:** search across sectors; do not prescribe one.
- **Technology:** do not force every problem into AI, software or automation.
- **Novelty:** freshness or transition delta can improve timing; novelty is not demand.
- **Transaction:** a useful unit of observation; pre-transaction, post-transaction, recurring operational and incident-driven problems remain admissible.
- **Cross-boundary:** a high-value subclass when ownership fragments; intra-team/intra-function problems are also admissible.
- **Regulation:** a trigger or barrier, not automatically a market. Do not claim regulated authority the entrant does not possess.
- **Physical manufacturability:** mandatory only for a physical product opportunity. For services/software, assess delivery, integration, reliability and support feasibility instead.
- **Existing capability:** execution advantage, never a reason to distort market selection.
- **Grants/credits:** an enabler, never proof that a customer needs or will pay for a product.

## 5. Run discovery and pre-kill simultaneously

Every search wave must actively seek both:
- evidence that a candidate is real, valuable and purchasable;
- evidence that it is already solved, internalized, commoditized, inaccessible, uneconomic, legally constrained or impossible to enter.

Do not first generate a long idea list and defer competitive analysis. Pre-kill the candidate while discovering it.

### Pre-kill checks

- Is the work already adequately handled by an internal employee or existing workflow?
- Is a supplier/competitor already solving it with little observable gap?
- Is it a low-price commodity with no defensible access or delivery advantage?
- Is the apparent beneficiary different from the actual buyer/payer?
- Is the buyer identifiable and is purchase authority plausible?
- Is the problem material and recurring/urgent enough to justify spend?
- Is an intervention legally and operationally permitted?
- Does it require regulated certification, professional authority, licenses or approvals we do not hold?
- Can the first transaction be delivered without building a speculative platform?
- Is working capital required, how much and for how long?
- Can the offer have bounded scope, objective acceptance and manageable liability?
- Can the first customer be reached and invoiced directly?
- Can payment be collected on a useful timescale?
- Does the delivery economics plausibly support the price?

Absence from a search result is not evidence of absence of competitors. Vendor presence is not proof that an unmet need exists. A job posting, market forecast, regulation or customer compliment is not a purchase commitment.

## 6. Keep market opportunity and entry feasibility separate

### Market opportunity

- Is the problem or need real?
- Is the economic consequence material?
- Is there a customer/payer pool?
- Is the need repeated, urgent or otherwise purchasable?
- Is the current market under-serving it?
- Is there evidence of actual spending or purchasing behavior?

### Entry feasibility for this user

- Is a specific first buyer reachable?
- Can the user deliver the first result with current skills/assets/partners?
- Can it be delivered before building a dedicated product?
- Is the legal and regulatory role permissible?
- Is the capital and working-capital exposure acceptable?
- Is liability bounded and insurable/contractually manageable?
- Can the first delivery be accepted and paid directly?
- Is time-to-cash short enough relative to the ticket and risk?

A large market opportunity may still be a poor entry path. A modest opportunity with a direct payer, short sales cycle and strong delivery economics may be a better first pursuit.

## 7. Hard gates for a first commercial entry

A demand-led candidate is not promoted to **ENTRY-CANDIDATE** unless:

1. A real problem/event is evidenced, not merely imagined.
2. Its consequence is material enough to matter.
3. A specific plausible legal entity/customer can be named as potential payer.
4. Buying authority is plausible and distinct from the beneficiary where necessary.
5. Current workaround, incumbents and internalization are understood.
6. No false regulated/official authority claim is needed.
7. The first intervention is bounded and has an objective deliverable.
8. The first delivery does not require speculative product construction.
9. Working-capital and liability exposure are manageable with available resources.
10. A reachable buyer/channel exists.
11. Acceptance can be decided objectively by an identified party.
12. Direct payment or a credible payment route can be tested.
13. Delivery economics plausibly support the transaction.

A candidate that fails a gate may remain an interesting **MARKET-OPPORTUNITY** or **RESEARCH-OPEN** item, but cannot be represented as an executable entry opportunity.

## 8. Preferred first-transaction shape

Prefer:

**Observed case → direct buyer → fixed/bounded scope → short delivery → objective acceptance → direct invoice/payment → post-mortem.**

Avoid:

**Build product → hope a market exists → acquire users → wait for network effects.**

Also avoid an entry path where payment depends on an unproven third party, a long approval chain or an unavailable working-capital facility.

Software/AI should be built only after the first delivery or repeated paid work reveals a stable reusable primitive. Reuse existing technical assets when they genuinely reduce delivery cost, but do not let the portfolio dictate the opportunity.

## 9. Separate evaluation tracks

### Track A — Demand-led market/entry opportunities

Use the hard gates above, then compare surviving candidates on:
- consequence/value at risk;
- frequency/urgency;
- strength of real demand evidence;
- current alternatives and size of the gap;
- identifiable payer and purchase authority;
- price and delivery economics;
- reachability and sales cycle;
- entry capital/liability;
- repeatability and defensibility;
- time-to-first-cash;
- expansion potential.

### Track B — Enablers/incentives

Require:
- official source and current terms;
- country/entity/founder/sector eligibility;
- benefit size actually consumable under expected use;
- exclusions and minimum spend/co-funding;
- expiry and drawdown schedule;
- data/IP/privacy and publicity obligations;
- payment/banking/tax constraints;
- application effort and approval probability evidence;
- integration, migration and lock-in cost;
- measurable net value after effort and restrictions.

Status must distinguish advertised, eligibility-checked, applied, approved, active, consumed, exhausted and expired.

### Track C — Asset/resource/investment opportunities

Require:
- verified ownership/rights and access;
- valuation and cash-flow basis;
- capital requirement and financing source;
- title/contract/tax/regulatory review;
- downside/liquidity/exit analysis;
- conflict-of-interest and counterparty checks;
- evidence of actual buyer/lessee/licensee or utilization.

### Track D — Research and knowledge opportunities

Require:
- exact research question and novelty boundary;
- primary literature/patent/prior-art review where relevant;
- reproducibility and independent falsification;
- access to required data/compute/lab/permissions;
- publication, licensing and IP ownership terms;
- a credible route to use, transfer, funding or measurable public value.

Do not confuse a scientifically interesting question with a commercial opportunity. Both can be valuable, but their success criteria differ.

## 10. Evidence model and non-claims

Use the portfolio's established claim labels:

- **ESTABLISHED** — supported under the applicable source/review standard.
- **EXPERIMENTALLY_SUPPORTED** — observed in a reproducible test or measured cohort.
- **LOCAL_MARKET_SIGNAL** — localized observation whose scope is narrower than market-wide demand.
- **USER_REPORTED** — reported by a user/operator, not independently verified.
- **INFERENCE** — derived interpretation with premises visible.
- **HYPOTHESIS** — proposition needing a discriminating test.
- **CONTRADICTED** — incompatible with stronger or more direct evidence.
- **UNKNOWN** — evidence insufficient.
- **OPEN** — unresolved issue or conflict.
- **KILLED** — candidate rejected under stated assumptions/evidence.
- **DEFERRED** — not pursued now due to a specified blocker or dependency.

For every material claim record:
- source URL/document or direct observation;
- publication/observation date and checked date;
- geography, population, segment, SKU or operating scope;
- evidence type and independence;
- what the evidence establishes and does not establish;
- contrary evidence;
- next test and falsifier.

Do not infer demand from TAM slides, search volume, views, likes, waitlists, downloads, vendor counts, job postings or broad macro growth without a causal/purchasing chain. These may be signals; record their limits.

Do not assign false-precision scores. Separate evidence strength from attractiveness. A high-impact candidate with UNKNOWN payer evidence remains blocked from commercial entry.

## 11. Candidate lifecycle and registers

Maintain three separate ledgers:

### Opportunity candidate register
`DISCOVERED → SCREENED → VERIFIED → ENTRY-CANDIDATE → TEST-READY → TESTED → PURSUED → REALIZED / KILLED / DEFERRED`

### Incentive/enabler register
`DISCOVERED → TERMS-VERIFIED → ELIGIBILITY-CHECKED → READY-TO-APPLY → APPLIED → APPROVED → ACTIVE → CONSUMED / EXHAUSTED / EXPIRED / REJECTED`

### Active experiment/transaction register
`HYPOTHESIS → BUYER-IDENTIFIED → SCOPE-AGREED → PRICE-QUOTED → PAID → DELIVERED → ACCEPTED / REJECTED → POST-MORTEM`

Every record should contain:
- stable candidate ID and date;
- opportunity type and geography;
- observed event/problem/benefit;
- affected party, economic sufferer, buyer, buying authority and payer;
- current handling, alternatives and pre-kill results;
- intervention and acceptance test;
- resources, capital, time-to-cash and liability;
- economics and assumptions;
- evidence refs and claim status;
- state, blocker, owner dependency and next action;
- explicit kill/reopen condition.

Preserve negative findings. Reopen a killed candidate only when a previously killed assumption materially changes (payer, trigger, consequence, competition, capital, regulation, channel or payment behavior). Do not restart by merely changing branding or implementation technology.

## 12. Discovery portfolio and geographic coverage

Search globally, but make geographic conclusions explicit. Evaluate separately:
- opportunity location and customer geography;
- user's possible entry location;
- cross-border supply, service, payment, tax, IP and data restrictions;
- founder/entity eligibility for programs;
- required local permits, licenses, certifications or representative roles;
- realistic reachability and time-to-cash.

Algeria is one important market and eligibility lens. It is not the exclusive boundary for discovery. Other geographies are admissible where access, legality, payment collection and execution are credible. Do not assume eligibility from nationality/residence alone; verify each program's current rules.

## 13. Portfolio fit is assessed after independent discovery

Only after a candidate survives market and entry screening ask:
- Can an existing repository, product, model, dataset or skill reduce first-delivery cost?
- Is the capability mature enough for the required obligation?
- Does reuse create unacceptable security, IP, reliability or regulatory coupling?
- Is a manual first delivery more informative and cheaper?
- Does the new opportunity justify a dedicated implementation?

A project fit is a leverage factor, not a demand signal. An opportunity that has no existing project fit must remain visible.

## 14. Required discovery wave output

Each discovery wave should report:
1. Search scope and areas actually examined.
2. Newly discovered candidates, with primary evidence and dates.
3. Candidates pre-killed and why.
4. Surviving market opportunities versus entry candidates.
5. Incentives/enablers that actually reduce cost/risk for a survivor.
6. Evidence status, confidence/data limitations and unresolved contradictions.
7. Economics, payer and payment mechanism where known.
8. The next discriminating test, its cost/effort and what result changes the decision.
9. Negative findings and explicit no-go decisions.
10. Changes since the prior wave; do not pad with stale candidates.

Do not manufacture an opportunity quota. A wave with no surviving candidate is a valid result.

## 15. Immediate operating rule

The correct default is:

**Explore broadly → observe economic reality → pre-kill incumbents/internalization/regulation → identify buyer/payer → test a bounded paid intervention → measure delivery economics → revalidate → only then build, integrate or scale.**

Do not begin with Medad, any existing repository, any sector, AI, a grant, or a fashionable trend. Those are all possible search findings, never the scope definition.
